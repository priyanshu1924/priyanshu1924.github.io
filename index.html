Mobile Robot Cart

Rolling base that carries a UFACTORY 850 arm, its control box and battery as one serviceable unit.

In development
  • Frame built from T-slot aluminum extrusion with bolted gusset plates and diagonal braces, so the structure can be re-cut and reconfigured instead of re-welded.
  • Top deck takes the arm mount; the bays underneath hold the DC control box and a 51.2 V, 20 Ah battery pack.
  • Leveling casters on bolted caster plates: roll it around the shop, then drop the feet so the cart doesn't creep while the arm works at full reach.
  • Bolt-on holders for the arm's control box and a laptop, plus a dedicated 850 mounting plate on the top deck.
  • Next step is a swerve-drive version: MK5i modules with NEO motors on a roboRIO 2.0, with a Raspberry Pi as the high-level controller.

Modular F1 / Rally Sim Wheel

A force-feedback sim racing wheel with the motor in the wheelbase and hot-swappable grips, built for less than an off-the-shelf direct-drive wheel.

Problem. Commercial force-feedback wheels are expensive, and switching between F1 and rally driving normally means a second wheel. The goal was one base, under $300 CAD in parts, with at least 5 N·m of force feedback and grips that swap in seconds.

  • Material. At full 500 W the hub motor runs at 80–120 °C. PLA creeps around its 50–60 °C glass transition, so the hub moved to polycarbonate.
  • Hot-swap grips. Fully printed grips snapped at the connection points under 5–15 N·m. A 3/8" aluminum sheet core now carries the load into the hub: 15 N·m at 0.1 m puts 150 N on each M5 hole.
  • Sensing. The motor's hall sensors were too coarse, so steering angle comes from a separate rotary encoder driven by an in-house GT2 timing pulley and tensioner.
  • Ergonomics. Button positions were set from feedback from 20 testers. Limit switches sit close to the hub so presses don't load the thin grip sections.
  • Next. PA-CF in the load path, a custom PCB for the inputs, and rotary encoders for brake bias and differential.

Cell-Level Fuse, MS16 Solar Car

Nickel-strip overcurrent protection for each 21700 cell in the MS16 battery pack, plus the thermal model and test rig used to tune it.

The pack is 5.25 kWh, built from 1s9p modules. Each cell gets its own fuse, so a single shorted cell drops out without taking the module with it. Fuses are 0.005" pure nickel (200/201), with the neck sized through a design of experiments.

I modelled blow time with a first-order heat balance. For a short circuit, conduction and convection are negligible, so the adiabatic form gives the melting I²t:

I²R₀(1+α(T−T₀)) − kA/L·(T−T_bus) − hA_s(T−T_amb) = m·c_p·dT/dt
∫I²dt = (D·c_p / ρ₀α) · ln(1+α(T_melt−T₀)) · A²
  • Validated on a rig with a 60 A shunt, current-limiting power resistors, a Keysight DMM and 4 AWG wire with copper blocks. Model agrees with measured blow times to within 10%.
  • Shortcomings found: deflection where the fuse meets both busbar and cell, risk of re-contact after blowing, and a shunt under-rated for 100 A runs.
  • Roadmap: a spring-style fuse that skips the busbar interface, a longer blow gap, and a 12 V 100 A supply with a 1000 W rheostat for testing.

Battery Module Design

A serviceable 21700 module for MS16 that can be swapped quickly and strung in series without rewiring the pack.

  • Designed the base module and the string layout that links modules in series.
  • Cell holders printed in PC-FR for fire resistance; the brace is a carbon-fiber sandwich panel.
  • Also designed the battery box mount to take 5 g acceleration loads, checked with SolidWorks FEA against FSAE crash standards.
  • Wrote a Python test rig (PyVISA) that drives a DMM and electronic load to measure cell capacity and internal resistance, which cut the cell scrap rate.
